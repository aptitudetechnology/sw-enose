# Whole-pathway computational models of early olfaction (receptor -> glomeruli -> sparse code -> readout)

Search method: PubMed E-utilities (esearch/esummary/efetch) for bibliographic records and abstracts; PMC full text (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for Luo 2010, Litwin-Kumar 2017, Stevens 2015 and Schaffer 2018; a hosted copy of the Dasgupta 2017 Science paper and its supplement (its.caltech.edu course copies). All titles, authors, years and venues below come from the PubMed record. Run 2026-11-15.

Stage key used in the tables: R = receptor binding/ORN responses; G = convergence of same-type ORNs onto glomeruli; A = adaptation; N = divisive normalisation / lateral inhibition; X = random sparse expansion; S = WTA / top-k / global-inhibition sparsening; L = learned readout.

## Q1. Fly models: ORN responses -> antennal-lobe normalisation -> Kenyon-cell sparse code

### Takeaway
Luo, Axel & Abbott 2010 is the closest published model to the sw-enose chain. It is driven by real Hallem & Carlson 2006 ORN data (20 receptors x 110 odorants). It goes through an Olsen-style normalisation with lateral suppression to 2,500 model KCs, each with about 5 random PN inputs and global inhibition. It does not model ORN-to-glomerulus convergence, adaptation or a learned KC readout. Dasgupta et al. 2017 and Stevens 2015 are abstractions of the same ORN -> PN -> KC -> APL top-5% chain. None of the fly models I confirmed has all seven sw-enose stages.

### Cited Findings
- **Luo SX, Axel R, Abbott LF. "Generating sparse and selective third-order responses in the olfactory system of the fly." PNAS 2010;107(23):10713-8.** PMID 20498080, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1005635107 (full text read, PMC2890779). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20498080/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2890779/)
  - The model input is the Hallem & Carlson dataset: "firing rates of ORNs expressing 20 different olfactory receptors ... to 110 different odorants". They used "responses generated by 20 nonspecialized receptors".
  - The ORN->PN transform is "a normalization model similar to what has been used in modeling visually responsive neurons". It "equalizes the magnitudes of and decorrelates responses to different odors through feedforward nonlinearities and lateral suppression" (abstract).
  - The KC layer has 2,500 model KCs, each receiving excitatory input from PNs "connected to five different glomeruli, along with global inhibition", with a common threshold giving about 5% sparseness. All 110 odors elicited responses in on average 125 cells.
  - Fan-in sweep: "the best performance seems to arise for between 5 and 15 PN inputs per KC", judged by missed odors and silent KCs. This is with N = 20 glomerular inputs.
  - Ablations: without the feedforward nonlinearity (raw ORN input), "∼30 odors are missing from the representation". Lateral suppression "plays a larger role in buffering the concentrat[ion]" effects. They also used the Hallem & Carlson concentration series (10 odorants x 4 concentrations).
  - Readout: there is no learned KC readout. The lateral-horn neurons get weights from Fisher's linear discriminant, which is a fitted readout but of PNs, not KCs.
  - Stages: R (real data, used directly as ORN rates), N, X, S, partial L. Not modelled: G (the data are already per receptor type) and A.
- **Stevens CF. "What the fly's nose tells the fly's brain." PNAS 2015;112(30):9460-5.** PMID 26150492, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1510103112 (full text read, PMC4522789). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26150492/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4522789/)
  - This is an analytical three-layer "encoder / decoder / user" theory. KCs combine "information from about six glomeruli, selected at random". The APL "winner-take-all circuit" lets about 5% of the most depolarised KCs fire, and these KCs form the odor "tag".
  - Its data input is PN firing rates from Bhandawat et al. (18 odors x 7 glomeruli), fitted with an exponential distribution, plus the Caron et al. glomerular sampling frequencies. It does not use Hallem & Carlson.
  - Stages: N (assumed, as a maximum-entropy code), X, S. There is no learned readout and no ORN or adaptation model.
- **Dasgupta S, Stevens CF, Navlakha S. "A neural algorithm for a fundamental computing problem." Science 2017;358(6364):793-796.** PMID 29123069, DOI 10.1126/science.aam9868 (full text and supplement read from a hosted copy: [main](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Dasgupta-2017.pdf), [supp](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Dasgupta-2017-supp.pdf)). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29123069/)
  - Circuit as stated: 50 ORN types -> 50 PNs. The first step "essentially 'centers the mean' ... using a technique called divisive normalization". "Fifty PNs project to 2000 Kenyon cells ... sparse, binary random connection matrix. Each KC receives and sums the firing rates from about six randomly selected PNs". The APL WTA keeps "the top 5%". The paper cites Hallem & Carlson 2006 for the exponential ORN rate distribution.
  - Evaluation used non-olfactory benchmarks: "SIFT (d = 128), GLOVE (d = 300), and MNIST (d = 784)", plus GIST in the supplement. It is framed as similarity search (locality-sensitive hashing), not as a chemical-sensing system. I saw no odor dataset used for evaluation.
  - Stages: N (mean-centring), X, S. There is no learned readout; the task is nearest-neighbour retrieval.
- **Endo K, Tsuchimoto Y, Kazama H. "Synthesis of Conserved Odor Object Representations in a Random, Divergent-Convergent Network." Neuron 2020;108(2):367-381.e5.** PMID 32814018, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2020.07.029 (abstract only; Europe PMC shows it as subscription access). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32814018/)
  - From the abstract: imaging of the antennal lobe -> MB transformation shows clustered representations for mixtures and groups of odors in the MB. These responses "all emerge in an experimentally well-constrained model implementing divergent-convergent, random connectivity". Decoding analyses show that mixture- and group-selective neurons improve generalisation. Relative odor representations are conserved across animals.
  - UNVERIFIED: whether the model input is the authors' own recorded PN/glomerular responses, and which stages it includes. I did not read the full text.
- **Litwin-Kumar et al. 2017** (see Q4) is the fan-in theory paper. Its inputs are synthetic (Gaussian or binary patterns), not receptor data.
- **Huerta R, Nowotny T, García-Sanchez M, Abarbanel HD, Rabinovich MI. "Learning classification in the olfactory system of insects." Neural Comput 2004;16(8):1601-40.** PMID 15228747, DOI 10.1162/089976604774201613 (abstract only). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15228747/)
  - It describes an AL -> KC expansion "into a higher-dimensional space" with no learning, followed by MB-lobe output neurons that classify linearly with "local Hebbian learning and by competition due to mutual inhibition". Stages: X, S (at the output), L. The input type is UNVERIFIED (not in the abstract).
- **Olsen SR, Bhandawat V, Wilson RI. "Divisive normalization in olfactory population codes." Neuron 2010.** PMID 20435004, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2010.04.009 (record only). This is the experimental basis for the normalisation stage. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20435004/)

### Inferences
- The fly field's "whole pathway" usually means ORN rates (often Hallem & Carlson) -> normalised PNs -> random sparse KCs -> WTA or threshold. Explicit ORN-to-glomerulus convergence and adaptation are generally left out, because Hallem & Carlson responses are already per receptor type. Among the papers checked, the sw-enose combination of receptor binding, convergence, adaptation and a learned readout in one chain is not matched by any single model.
- In Luo 2010, about 5 inputs per KC is 25% of the 20 glomeruli used. In Dasgupta and Litwin-Kumar, 6-7 inputs is 12-14% of 50. So the published "fan-in ≈ 6" is tied to the input dimension each model assumes.

### Gaps
- I did not read Endo et al. 2020 in full, so its stages and data source are unconfirmed.
- I did not check DoOR-driven KC models specifically (no targeted search), so I cannot say whether a DoOR-driven end-to-end fly model exists.
- I did not check connectome-constrained whole-pathway models (hemibrain or FlyWire based) beyond one 2026 arXiv preprint (see Q3).

## Q2. Mammalian equivalents: olfactory bulb -> piriform

### Takeaway
The confirmed mammalian whole-pathway models (Schaffer et al. 2018; Babadi & Sompolinsky 2014 in general form) use synthetic glomerular odor patterns, not receptor data. Schaffer et al. include a random bulb -> piriform expansion and a learned linear readout. I found no mammalian OB -> piriform model driven by real receptor-response data in these searches.

### Cited Findings
- **Schaffer ES, Stettler DD, Kato D, Choi GB, Axel R, Abbott LF. "Odor Perception on the Two Sides of the Brain: Consistency Despite Randomness." Neuron 2018;98(4):736-742.e3.** PMID 29706585, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2018.04.004 (full text read, PMC6026547). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29706585/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6026547/)
  - Abstract: "Neurons in piriform cortex receive input from a random collection of glomeruli". Combining imaging and modelling, they show that "correlations are retained in the transformation from bulb to piriform", and that "consistent generalization may require the full complement of piriform neurons."
  - Input: "model odors in the bulb" with a tunable "excess overlap parameter f" (f = 0, 0.3, 0.7), so the odors are synthetic. Readout: "a linear readout ... z = Σ w_i y_i", trained, with readout weights set to the piriform response to the trained odor (Hebbian-like). The paper also compares against MBON data (Hige et al. 2015b).
  - Stages: X, threshold sparsening, L. There is no receptor, adaptation or normalisation model.
- **Stettler DD, Axel R. "Representations of odor in the piriform cortex." Neuron 2009.** PMID 19778513, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2009.09.005 (record only, abstract not read). This is primarily an imaging study. UNVERIFIED whether it contains a model. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19778513/)
- **Babadi B, Sompolinsky H. "Sparseness and expansion in sensory representations." Neuron 2014;83(5):1213-26.** PMID 25155954, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2014.07.035 (abstract only). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25155954/)
  - Inputs are clustered synthetic patterns. Random feedforward expansion "amplifies variability in the incoming stimuli, and this noise enhancement increases with sparseness". "The low dimensionality of the input layer generates overlaps between the induced representations of different stimuli, limiting the benefit of expansion." The paper discusses olfactory, cerebellar and visual implications. Stages: X, S, L (classification).
- Dasgupta et al. 2017 Table 1 lists mouse olfaction as "Olfactory bulb; 1000 glomeruli / Dense, weak; samples all glomeruli / Piriform cortex; 100,000 semi-lunar cells" ([main text](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Dasgupta-2017.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Babadi & Sompolinsky's point that low input dimension limits the benefit of expansion applies directly to an 8-channel array: no expansion can create more independent dimensions than the input supplies.

### Gaps
- I did not search for OB -> piriform models driven by mouse OR response data (for example Saito et al. 2009 receptor-ligand screens) or for OB models with mitral/granule lateral inhibition chained to piriform. Imam & Cleland (below) is an OB-only model.

## Q3. Whole-pathway models framed as e-nose / chemical-sensor processing

### Takeaway
Yes, there is a clear prior lineage that uses the insect pathway explicitly as an e-nose or chemical-data classifier. Schmuker & Schneider 2007 (virtual receptors -> lateral inhibition -> classifier) and Schmuker, Pfeil & Nawrot 2014 (virtual receptors -> lateral inhibition -> WTA with supervised learning, on neuromorphic hardware) are two examples. Diamond et al. 2016 (antennal-lobe-inspired spiking classifier on real MOX e-nose time series) is a third. Imam & Cleland 2020 is a fourth, for the mammalian OB on Loihi with real chemosensor-array data. None of these is described in its abstract as having the full chain including a random KC-like sparse expansion with top-k. Huerta et al. 2004 has the expansion but the abstract does not say it was applied to sensor data.

### Cited Findings
- **Schmuker M, Schneider G. "Processing and classification of chemical data inspired by insect olfaction." PNAS 2007;104(51):20285-9.** PMID 18077325, DOI 10.1073/pnas.0705683104 (abstract). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18077325/)
  - The method has three steps. "Virtual receptors" sample stimulus space and are arranged by a self-organising map. Their signals are "decorrelated via correlation-based lateral inhibition". An ML classifier comes last. "Signal decorrelation during the second stage significantly increases the accuracy". It was applied to molecular descriptors and bioactivity prediction.
- **Schmuker M, Pfeil T, Nawrot MP. "A neuromorphic network for generic multivariate data classification." PNAS 2014;111(6):2081-6.** PMID 24469794, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1303053111 (abstract). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24469794/)
  - Virtual receptors convert data into spikes, followed by "lateral inhibition and drives a winner-take-all circuit that supports supervised learning". It runs on accelerated neuromorphic hardware, and performance was "on par with a naïve Bayes classifier" on real-world datasets.
- **Diamond A, Schmuker M, Berna AZ, Trowell S, Nowotny T. "Classifying continuous, real-time e-nose sensor data using a bio-inspired spiking network modelled on the insect olfactory system." Bioinspir Biomim 2016;11(2):026002.** PMID 26891474, DOI 10.1088/1748-3190/11/2/026002 (abstract). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891474/)
  - It is an AL-inspired "inhibition and competition" classifier on metal-oxide sensor time series, with 20 odours. "Four out of twelve available sensors and the first 30 s ... are sufficient to deliver 92% accurate classification without access to an odour onset signal."
- **Imam N, Cleland TA. "Rapid online learning and robust recall in a neuromorphic olfactory circuit." Nat Mach Intell 2020;2(3):181-191.** PMID 38650843, DOI 10.1038/s42256-020-0159-4 (abstract). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38650843/)
  - It is a mammalian OB-architecture spiking algorithm on Intel Loihi that learns odour representations "from the activity of chemosensor arrays mounted in a wind tunnel", with one-shot learning, neuromodulation and neurogenesis. It is OB-only; I saw no piriform expansion in the abstract.
- **Huerta et al. 2004** (Neural Comput, PMID 15228747, abstract): AL -> KC expansion followed by a Hebbian-learned linear classifier with mutual inhibition. The abstract frames it as insect odor classification. The authors later worked on e-nose applications, but I did not confirm those in this search.
- **Dasgupta et al. 2017** reframes the fly chain as a computing algorithm (LSH), but for image and word-vector similarity search, not chemical sensing.
- UNVERIFIED lead: Senge S., "A Connectome Test of the Fly Hashing Algorithm", arXiv:2610.09114v1 (6 Oct 2026), seen only via an alphaxiv listing page ([link](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.09114v1)). The listing says it tests the Dasgupta model against four EM connectomes. Gaussian projections "given the same projection arithmetic retrieve better on every dataset". Measured glomerular wiring gives "no consistent retrieval advantage over degree-preserving random rewiring (median change -1.6%)". The odour data source is not named on the listing. I did not open the arXiv record itself.

### Inferences
- An insect-inspired e-nose processing pipeline is well-established prior art (2007 onwards). What may be distinctive about sw-enose is the combination of stages, especially simulated receptor binding with same-type convergence and adaptation ahead of the KC expansion. I could not confirm that from abstracts alone.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full texts of Schmuker 2007/2014 or Diamond 2016, so their exact stages beyond the abstracts (for example whether any uses a random KC expansion) are unconfirmed.
- I did not search for other bio-inspired e-nose work from Nowotny, Huerta or Marco, or for MOX + mushroom-body papers in IEEE or Sensors journals.

## Q4. Fan-in scaling: KC fan-in relative to number of glomeruli

### Takeaway
Litwin-Kumar et al. 2017 analyse exactly this question. The optimal fan-in K is not an absolute constant. It depends on the number of inputs N (and on M, the coding level f and the weight distribution). The fly value K ≈ 7 comes out of N = 50 glomeruli and M = 2,000 KCs. Their theory implies that K = 6 on N = 8 inputs is far outside the useful regime, although they did not simulate N = 8 (that conclusion is my inference). Dasgupta's supplement explicitly treats fan-in as a fraction of the inputs (about 10%) when moving the fly algorithm to other datasets.

### Cited Findings
- **Litwin-Kumar A, Harris KD, Axel R, Sompolinsky H, Abbott LF. "Optimal Degrees of Synaptic Connectivity." Neuron 2017;93(5):1153-1164.e7.** PMID 28215558, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2017.01.030 (full text read, PMC5379477). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28215558/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5379477/)
  - Abstract: "the dimensions of the cerebellar granule-cell and Drosophila Kenyon-cell representations are maximized at degrees of synaptic connectivity that match those observed anatomically ... When input synapses are subject to supervised plasticity, however, dense wiring becomes advantageous."
  - Combinatorial ("birthday") criterion: p is the probability that all M neurons choose distinct input sets from R = C(N, K) possibilities, and "K* [is] the smallest value of K for which p attains at least 95% of its maximum". "Using numbers appropriate for the mushroom body, N = 50 and M = 2000, leads to K* = 7, equal to the average observed number of projection-neuron inputs ... (p takes the values 0.88, 0.98, and 0.996 for K = 6, 7, and 8)."
  - Dimension: "The dimension is maximized for an intermediate value of K that depends on N, f, and the distribution of synaptic weights. For the case of homogeneous excitatory synaptic weights, this value is K = 9 for N = 1000 and f = 0.1. Above this value, dimension decreases because of positive average correlations among the mixed-layer neurons."
  - With balancing inhibition the dimension peak moves to K = N/2. Even so, "it attains 95% of its maximum value at K = 29, or approximately 3% connectivity" for N = 1000.
  - Fixed wiring budget S = MK: "For f = 0.1 and S = 14,000, consistent with the Drosophila mushroom body ... the optimum occurs at K = 4 when inhibition is absent or K = 8 when it is present ..., close to the observed value of 7." With broadly distributed weights, "the optimal value of K ... is no more than 7".
  - Dimension saturation: "The limiting value scales linearly with N ... expansion ratios beyond 10–50 do not increase the mixed-layer dimension."
  - Inputs in the theory are synthetic ("for now, we take [input patterns] to be standard Gaussians"; binary and spike-count variants appear in Fig. 6/S4). The paper is not driven by receptor data.
- **Dasgupta et al. 2017, supplement** ([PDF](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~jkenny/nb250c/papers/Dasgupta-2017-supp.pdf)): "In the fly, each KC samples from roughly 10% of the PNs (6 out of 50). We tried setting this value to 1% and to 50% ... Across all datasets, the most consistent performance was obtained when sampling 10%, with no improvement in performance at 50%." The text refers readers to Litwin-Kumar et al. for "how sampling affects associative learning". For SIFT, GLOVE and MNIST (d = 128-784), the fan-in was therefore set as a fraction of d, not as an absolute 6.
- **Luo et al. 2010** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2890779/)): with N = 20 Hallem & Carlson receptor inputs, "the best performance seems to arise for between 5 and 15 PN inputs per KC".

### Inferences (mine, not stated by any of these papers for N = 8)
- The birthday criterion applied to sw-enose: with N = 8 and K = 6, R = C(8,6) = 28 distinct input sets. Even the best case, K = 4, gives only C(8,4) = 70. With hundreds or thousands of KCs, most KCs must be exact duplicates, so p is essentially 0 for every K. The 8-input array cannot reach Litwin-Kumar's regime at all.
- Under random sampling, two KCs with K = 6 of N = 8 share on average K²/N = 4.5 of their 6 inputs (overlap fraction K/N = 0.75). That produces exactly the "positive average correlations among the mixed-layer neurons" which Litwin-Kumar et al. identify as what collapses dimension above the optimum. This fits sw-enose's finding that transplanting the absolute fly fan-in of 6 destroyed discrimination. The fly's 6/50 (12%) and Luo's 5/20 (25%) are low-overlap regimes. 6/8 (75%) is not.
- A direct proportional transplant of the fly ratio (about 10-14%) to N = 8 gives K < 1. Together with Babadi & Sompolinsky's point that low input dimension caps the expansion benefit, this suggests that for very small arrays the useful fan-in sits at the low end (K = 1-3), and that expansion alone cannot add discriminative dimensions beyond the roughly 8 the array provides. This is inference and should be checked empirically.
- None of the confirmed papers analyses N as small as 8. The specific failure sw-enose observed is predicted by the Litwin-Kumar framework but not reported in it.

### Gaps
- I did not read Litwin-Kumar's Figure S1 (dimension vs K for small N) or the Methods formula for dim(m) as a function of N and K closely enough to give a computed optimum for N = 8. It could be computed from their Equation 3.
- I did not search for later papers extending fan-in theory to small input dimension (for example follow-ups from the Litwin-Kumar or Abbott groups).
- Krishnamurthy K, Hermundstad AM, Mora T, Walczak AM, Balasubramanian V. "Disorder and the Neural Representation of Complex Odors." Front Comput Neurosci 2022, PMID 36003684, DOI 10.3389/fncom.2022.917786: I saw the record only. It is likely relevant to expansion and sparsening, but I did not assess it.
