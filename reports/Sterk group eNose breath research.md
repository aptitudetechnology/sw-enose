# Amsterdam breathprints shrink when tested honestly

Over nearly two decades, Peter Sterk's group at AMC/Amsterdam UMC moved through three devices. First came a commercial carbon black-polymer Cyranose 320 that read Tedlar breath bags (2007 onward). Next was a four-instrument "assembly of eNoses" running on sorbent tubes for the multi-site U-BIOPRED asthma project. Last came the in-house SpiroNose: metal-oxide (MOS) sensor arrays mounted behind a spirometer, with a duplicate set of reference sensors that sample room air at the same time. The SpiroNose is now sold by the start-up Breathomix BV, whose COO is the device's lead developer. Two engineering ideas from this work transfer directly to a low-cost build. The first is the ambient-reference "alveolar gradient" subtraction pipeline. The second is a manoeuvre-anchored feature set built on ratios to one stable sensor. The bigger lesson is about validation. Accuracy figures of 90-100% in early studies, which were cross-validated on 30-90 subjects, fell steadily as designs became more honest. In one clear case, a published lung-cancer model scored AUC 0.92 in its target COPD population and 0.80 in a broader prospective cohort. The centre-held-out ILD validation and the leased-device COVID-19 rollout showed site effects as well. In the rollout, Dutch government verification found that the agreed sensitivity was not met, and the ministry stated that Breathomix did not share the requested data. These searches found no fully independent replication of a Sterk-group model. All processing was conventional statistics. None was biologically inspired. This report covers engineering and methodology only. It gives no diagnostic or treatment advice for any individual.

Source-status conventions used throughout: **[A]** means only the PubMed/Europe PMC abstract was read. **[FT]** means full text was retrieved and searched or read. **UNVERIFIED** marks items the notes could not confirm from a primary source. Breathomix web pages and the Breathomix "List of Publications" (company document v33, 2 Feb 2026, local file `/home/chris/sw-enose/reports/20260202-V33-Breathomix-List-of-Publications.pdf`) are treated as leads and manufacturer claims, not evidence.

## Four phases trace a path from bags to the cloud

The first phase (2007-2013) used the Cyranose 320. These were small case-control studies with breath collected in Tedlar bags, analysed by PCA followed by linear or canonical discriminant analysis, and reported as cross-validated accuracy (CVV). By 2011 the group was testing new patients against an earlier training set. The second phase (2013-2019) was U-BIOPRED. There the group merged signals from four eNoses of different sensor technologies, run centrally on thermally desorbed Tenax tubes, and wrote up multi-site methodology. The third phase (2015 onward) introduced the SpiroNose to bring eNose analysis to the "point-of-care" through routine spirometry ([PubMed 26469298](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26469298/)). The fourth phase (2019-2026) brought large SpiroNose cohorts (BreathCloud) and external validation, led more and more by other centres such as Erasmus MC (ILD), NKI (lung cancer), Toronto (CF) and LUMC (post-COVID). Sterk's own authorship thins out after about 2023 in these searches.

The device credit tells the commercial story. Papers credit "SpiroNose (Amsterdam UMC, Amsterdam, the Netherlands)" in 2020/21 ([PMC7792783](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC7792783)), "SpiroNose; Breathomix" in 2023 ([PMC10635840](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10635840)), and "Breathomix, Leiden" in 2026 ([PubMed 41881763](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41881763/)). No source states why the group moved away from the Cyranose. The likely reasons, inferred from the papers' stated aims, are: removing bag and transport steps, standardising the manoeuvre through the spirometer, correcting for ambient air, and the "incompatibility between eNoses" the group itself had flagged in 2013 ([PubMed 23786277](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23786277/)).

## The SpiroNose pipeline: two arrays, subtraction, then ratios

### Sensors: what is published and what is only claimed

The 2015 prototype is fully described in the founding paper. It had five identical arrays, each holding four **Figaro (Japan) thick-film SnO2 MOS sensors**. Three arrays sat inside the tube in the breath path and two sat outside it as ambient references, all "at the rear end of a pneumotachograph." Figaro sensors were chosen "based on their good long-term performance and stability," citing a review by Romain & Nicolas (2010). AMC co-developed the device with Comon Invent BV, Delft. Its output was "changes in electrical voltage," sent in real time by a GSM module to a Comon Invent server ([JBR2015 PDF](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1752-7155/9/4/046001/pdf)). The quoted "−40 °C to +70 °C" operating range is almost certainly the datasheet ambient range, not a heater set-point. **No heater temperature, heater modulation, sampling rate, ADC resolution or tube geometry is published in anything read**, so constant-temperature operation is a plausible inference but UNVERIFIED.

From 2018 the device used **seven different MOS sensor types**, each present in duplicate on both the breath side and the reference side. The ERJ 2018 supplement describes this as "8 separate sensor arrays, 4 reference ... and 4 ... monitor[ing] the VOCs in exhaled breath" ([thesis Ch. 4](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066326/Chapter_4.pdf)). Breathomix's product page instead says "28 sensors arranged in four sets of seven" ([Breathomix](https://www.breathomix.com/?p=554)). The note-taker reads both as the same 28 sensors. **The manufacturer and model of the seven later sensors were not found**. Figaro TGS-series is plausible but UNVERIFIED. The company's statement that the sensors were chosen as "the sensors with the highest ability to distinguish between different (lung) diseases" has no published data or reference behind it. It is **a manufacturer claim only**, and nothing shows whether that selection was made independently of later validation data. The company timeline of prototypes (2014: 2 USB sensors; 2015: 6 MOS; 2016: 7 MOS; then cloud-connected) conflicts with the published 2015 design of four sensor types across five arrays ([Breathomix timeline](https://breathomix.com/?p=1853)). The published record should take precedence.

Before the SpiroNose, the U-BIOPRED platform merged 158 signals: Comon Invent MOS (8), Cyranose (32), Owlstone Lonestar FAIMS (110) and Tor Vergata metalloporphyrin-coated QMB (8). All were read from Tenax tubes thermally desorbed into Tedlar bags with N2 ([Brinkman thesis](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/42598554/Thesis.pdf); [PMC5710949](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5710949)). Lazar 2010 confirms "32 carbon black polymer sensors" for the Cyranose ([PMC3230982](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3230982/)). Another note had left that figure UNVERIFIED, but this full-text source settles it.

### The alveolar-gradient subtraction, step by step

Ambient correction is **reference subtraction, not ratio or regression modelling**. "Alveolar gradients were obtained by subtracting inspiratory (reference signal) from expiratory VOC concentrations (patient signal)." The published sequence has five steps. Low-pass Butterworth-filter the reference signals. Remove linear trends by least-squares fit. Average the two reference arrays. Time-align the averaged reference to each breath signal by cross-correlation, "as both showed a similar but delayed pattern." Subtract it from the low-pass-filtered breath signal, then remove residual linear trends and offsets. The three corrected breath signals "were not averaged" ([JBR2015](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1752-7155/9/4/046001/pdf)). In 2018 one step was added at the front: "removal of the SpiroNose's eigenfrequency" ([thesis Ch. 4](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066326/Chapter_4.pdf)). The method for that step and the frequency itself are not given, so a notch filter is only a guess (UNVERIFIED). **Butterworth order, cutoff, sampling rate and the cross-correlation lag window are not reported.**

The published effect is real but internal. Before correction, the three in-tube arrays differed significantly for sensor 1 (p < 0.001) and sensor 3 (p < 0.05), even though they sampled the same breath. After correction they did not differ, and reproducibility reached Cohen's kappa 0.81-0.94. The authors also admitted that two reference arrays "might have been overly cautious" ([JBR2015](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1752-7155/9/4/046001/pdf)). De Vries's thesis presents the method as a replacement for the VOC-filter "wash-out" approach, for which "it is not known how long such a wash-out period should last." The thesis says the correction "significantly improved" discrimination and cut measurement time ([thesis Ch. 8](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066334/Chapter_8.pdf)). Whether today's BreathBase cloud algorithm still matches the 2015/2018 description is not documented.

### Breath manoeuvre and flow dependence

The setup has a mouthpiece, nose clip and viral/bacterial filter on a clinical PFT system, with **no inspiratory VOC filter**. In 2015 the manoeuvre was 10 tidal breaths, an inspiratory capacity manoeuvre, a 5 s breath-hold, and then a vital-capacity exhalation at **< 0.4 L/s**. Patients rinsed their mouths three times, and the measurement was done in duplicate 2 min apart. From 2018 onward the protocol used 5 tidal breaths and slow maximal exhalation to residual volume ([JBR2015](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1752-7155/9/4/046001/pdf); [Chest2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10635840/)). Adding the SpiroNose did not affect spirometry (p > 0.5). Discrimination was retained with the bacterial filter in place.

Flow is the dominant physical confounder. **Raw sensor deflection correlated with expiratory flow at R² = 0.84** in 60 healthy subjects. The group found no correlation with exhaled volume, humidity or temperature. The authors recommend 0.19-0.38 L/s, note that MOS sensors "likely become saturated" in that range, and concede that flow "can only be influenced by instructing the patient," for example through visual pneumotach feedback ([JBR2015](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1752-7155/9/4/046001/pdf)). One extracted passage reports absolute values "not significantly influenced by the expiratory flow rate (p > 0.5)" within the tested range. That sits awkwardly beside the R² = 0.84 result, and the notes describe the extraction as fragmentary, so this point is unresolved. **No dead-space discard, CO2 gating or automatic out-of-range-flow rejection rule is published.** Respir Res 2023 removes measurements for "wrong breathing technique or unstable ambient air" but gives no criteria ([PMC10626662](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10626662/)). The Breathomix "approximately 30 seconds" measurement time is a manufacturer claim ([Breathomix](https://www.breathomix.com/?p=554)). The Cyranose-era protocol it replaced was 5 min of tidal breathing through an A2 VOC filter, then a vital capacity into a 10-L Tedlar bag sampled within 10 min alongside a room-air bag, with the "first sniff" discarded ([PMC3230982](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3230982/)).

### Features: two scalars per sensor, normalised to a reference sensor

The features are very compact. After filtering, subtraction and detrending, a Matlab local-maxima finder locates the peak. The **highest peak of the duplicate measurements is normalised to one reference sensor**: sensor 4 ("most sensitive," 1-30 ppm) in 2015 and sensor 2 ("most stable") from 2018. The authors' stated reason: "sensor-to-sensor ratios rather than absolute sensor values, to minimize inter-array differences" ([JBR2015](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1752-7155/9/4/046001/pdf)). In 2018 they added a **peak / breath-hold-point ratio**, which "is influenced to a larger extend by the sensor signal obtained during the previous 5 tidal breaths." That gives 14 peaks plus 14 ratios, 28 variables in all ([thesis Ch. 4](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066326/Chapter_4.pdf)). Later papers drop the reference sensor's constant normalised peak and report "13 values per measurement" ([PMC10626662](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10626662/)). No rise-time, area or curve-shape descriptors are used. The criterion for "most stable" is not given, and neither are peak-finder parameters or how the breath-hold point is timed. A single-subject series of 48 months of daily data, smoothed with a moving average, showed "changes in the ratios between the various sensors" over time ([thesis Ch. 8](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066334/Chapter_8.pdf)).

### Quality-control gas and drift

Before every session, or every morning, the SpiroNose was flushed with the hospital's **standard pulmonary-diffusion test gas**: acetylene, CO and CH4 at 0.300 ± 0.015% each, O2 20.7%, balance N2, at about 3 L/s for 5 s. The authors concede a flow below 0.4 L/s "would have been most optimal" ([JBR2015](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1752-7155/9/4/046001/pdf)). The thesis reports that ambient-corrected values "showed no sensor drift during the different studies using this QC gas," with a "5.2 to 7.4-fold higher effect size" than sensor variability. It also acknowledges that "sensor drift is not entirely deterministic," that a breath-mimicking QC gas is the needed next step, and that doubts persist over "limited sensor stability, the inability to calibrate devices simultaneously, and the difficulty of mass-producing identical sensors" ([thesis Ch. 8](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066334/Chapter_8.pdf)). In the published methods the QC gas **checks sensor function; it is not used to compute per-device correction factors**. No published method for calibrating many SpiroNose units to each other was found. Breathomix's "cloud-based calibration to ensure consistent, standardized data across all devices" is undocumented (UNVERIFIED manufacturer claim, [Breathomix](https://www.breathomix.com/?p=554)). The thesis claim of "portability of algorithms between devices" rests on within-device array comparisons and a two-site comparison in the 2015 paper.

### Statistics and classifiers

The house method runs from the Cyranose through the SpiroNose. Fit PCA and keep components with eigenvalue > 1 (Kaiser criterion). Screen components with ANOVA or t-tests, bootstrap-validated (1,000 iterations in Chest 2023). Then apply linear or canonical discriminant analysis with leave-one-out cross-validation, followed by ROC. From 2018 a random 2:1 train/validation split was used, and **PCs were fitted on training data only** ([thesis Ch. 4](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066326/Chapter_4.pdf); [Chest2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10635840/)). U-BIOPRED used unsupervised Ward/SIMPROF, K-means, PAM and topological data analysis ([PubMed 30529449](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30529449/)). Collaborators added PLS-DA (mixOmics), gradient boosting, adaptive LASSO and sPLS-DA, mostly without reporting hyperparameters. A random forest with feature selection under nested cross-validation scored best on sarcoidosis data (accuracy 87.1%, AUC 91.2%; [PubMed 37595574](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37595574/)). The group's own thesis warns that conclusions "based on a single classification model" are "prone to bias and overfitting." It adds that because SpiroNose variables from the same sensor overlap, ML models "will be more likely to overfit" ([thesis Ch. 8](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066334/Chapter_8.pdf)). The group's 2015 classifier re-analysis found that "no single combination of dimension reduction and classification methods gave consistent results" ([PubMed 26669708](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26669708/)). Validation design mattered more than the choice of classifier.

| van Raaij 2023 | 135 post-COVID + 174 controls | No; leave-one-out | AUC 0.893; no separation by diffusion capacity or CT | [PubMed 37081422](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37081422/) |
| de Vries 2023 (BreathCloud) | 682 COPD, 211 lung cancer; 37 later cancers | Internal | AUC 0.90 at baseline for later lung cancer | [PMC10635840](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10635840) [FT] |
| Licht 2023 | 100 CF children | Not stated | Any pathogen vs none AUC 0.791 | [PubMed 36849333](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36849333/) [A] |
| **Buma 2025** | 364, 2 Dutch sites, prospective | **Yes: published model applied prospectively** | Original model **0.92 in COPD, 0.80 in all participants**; at 95% sensitivity, specificity 72% (COPD) vs 51% (all); new model validation AUC 0.83 | [PubMed 40174676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40174676/) [A] |
| **Formsma 2026** | 587, 5 international centres | **Yes: whole centres held out** | Subtype vs other ILD 0.88-0.92 → 0.75-0.95; between subtypes 0.95-0.98 → 0.83-0.93 | [PubMed 41738130](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41738130/) [A] |

Three patterns stand out. First, "external" in the group's own papers usually means a later or randomly split set from the same programme (Fens 2011; de Vries 2019). Only Formsma 2026 holds out whole centres, and only Buma 2025 tests a previously published model on prospective data. Second, healthy controls make discrimination easy. ILD vs 48 healthy controls gave AUC 1.00, but comparisons between groups clinicians can actually confuse land at 0.75-0.93. Third, the Buma 2025 cohort had a disease prevalence of 59-84%, far above any screening setting. The training-set AUC of the original COPD model that Buma tested was not found. The three letters to the editor and the author reply were not read, so whatever criticism they contain is not represented here.

The group's own Leopold 2015 re-analysis quantifies this pattern. Only **7 of 46** eNose studies up to 2014 used external validation. Moving from in-set to leave-one-out performance cut ROC-AUC by **0.10-0.15** (−0.15, −0.14, −0.10, −0.11). External validation cut it by a further **0.23** in dataset 1 and 0.09 in dataset 3. Datasets 2 and 4 rose slightly (+0.07, +0.04). Risk of bias was high in most studies because patients were not randomly selected ([PubMed 26669708](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26669708/)). The external loss is real but not predictable in size.

## Field evidence and reviews temper the published numbers

### Systematic reviews: high pooled accuracy, weak evidence base

Scheepers et al. (JAMA Netw Open 2022; DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.19372; PMC9244610; first ~100k characters of full text read) pooled 52 cancer eNose studies covering 3677 patients. Pooled sensitivity was 90%, specificity 87% and AUC 94%, and the Cyranose 320 subgroup reached 93%/89%. But **"all studies were feasibility studies."** The index-test domain was at high risk of bias in 83%, flow and timing in 54%, and patient selection in 40%. Specificity heterogeneity was I² = 95.53%, and the Deeks funnel test was significant (P = .02). Only 58% of studies reported fasting, 46% smoking cessation and 10% a rest period. The review concludes that "adequately powered external validation studies ... are rarely conducted." The 2015 SpiroNose paper is included with AUC 95% and accuracy 88% for lung cancer ([PMC9244610](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9244610/)). Other reviews read at abstract level give similar pooled figures. In COPD, sensitivity was 83% and specificity 78% (eNose specificity 0.87; Chai 2025, PMID 41316246, PMC12860002). In COVID-19, Subali 2022 (PMID 34879323) reported 98.2%/74.3% and Long 2024 (PMID 38692202) reported 0.92/0.90. In cancer, Neugut & Neugut 2026 (PMID 41604309, PMC12923108) reported 85.9%/83.6% ([PubMed 41316246](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41316246/); [PubMed 34879323](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34879323/); [PubMed 38692202](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38692202/); [PubMed 41604309](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41604309/)). Given the heterogeneity and funnel asymmetry, these pooled figures are best read as upper bounds. Neither COVID abstract mentions the Dutch field outcome below. The 2017 ERS technical standard, co-authored by Sterk, notes there was previously no official standard for exhaled VOCs and "is not intended to provide clinical guidance." Its eNose-specific recommendations could not be read, so their wording is UNVERIFIED ([PubMed 28446552](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28446552/)). No Cochrane review was found. A colorectal-cancer review (10.3389/fonc.2024.1397259) and a Steenhuis 2025 lung-cancer review were seen only as search hits (UNVERIFIED, not opened).

### Drift and confounders

The clearest drift finding comes from an Amsterdam UMC paediatric/GI group working on faecal samples with the Cyranose 320, not the breath group. Bosch et al. (Sensors 2022; PMID 36501947; PMC9740993; [A]) report that "sensor data displayed associations with date of measurement, which was reproducible across all samples," and that drift correction "is not routinely performed" ([PubMed 36501947](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36501947/)). The SpiroNose's no-drift claim rests on the group's own QC-gas data. Confounding by design is visible in the early work. In Dragonieri 2007, age and disease were linked: young mild asthma was compared with older severe asthma. In de Vries 2015, asthma patients and controls averaged about 41 years while COPD and lung-cancer patients averaged 63-66. In U-BIOPRED, clusters differed by oral-corticosteroid use (P = .035), so medication is tangled up with phenotype ([PubMed 26469298](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26469298/); [PubMed 30529449](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30529449/)). Fens 2011 reports accuracy "not confounded by current smoking," but Fens 2009 found COPD breathprints partially overlapped with those of asymptomatic smokers ([PubMed 21732998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21732998/)). A Bikov 2014 finding that "expiratory flow, breath hold and dead space influence" eNose patterns came only from a search summary (10.1186/1471-2466-14-202; UNVERIFIED). In the other direction, Lazar 2010 showed that methacholine-induced bronchoconstriction did not change breathprints relative to saline sham, but nebulised aerosol itself did ([PMC3230982](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3230982/)).

### The Dutch COVID-19 field verification

This was the largest real-world deployment, and the most independent test, of a SpiroNose model. The ministry's answer to parliamentary questions (Aanhangsel Handelingen TK 2021-2022, nr. 1818; answer by Minister Kuipers received 22 Feb 2022; full text read) gives these facts. The state leased **"1800 apparaten"** until 1 July 2022. The test was "medio februari tijdelijk stopgezet naar aanleiding van een aantal mogelijke foute uitslagen": temporarily halted in mid-February after a number of possibly incorrect results. The note inserts the year "[2021]" in brackets; that year is the note-taker's addition and is UNVERIFIED here. The answer continues that "uit de robuuste verificatie gebleken dat de afgesproken sensitiviteit niet werd behaald. Ook op de specificiteit bleef de ademtest achter": robust verification showed the agreed sensitivity was not achieved, and specificity also fell short. On 6 September 2021 the OMT advised against the test "in de huidige vorm" (in its current form). And "Aan Breathomix is herhaaldelijk gevraagd om alle beschikbare data te delen, maar deze heeft Breathomix niet verstrekt": Breathomix was repeatedly asked to share all available data and did not. **The answer gives no sensitivity or specificity numbers** ([officielebekendmakingen](https://zoek.officielebekendmakingen.nl/ah-tk-20212022-1818.odt)). The research notes say the contract was terminated, but the quoted ministry text does not itself contain a termination statement, so treat that detail as resting on the notes' summary.

A Landelijk Expertteam Ademtest letter (WOO release, undated, responding to an email of 2 March [2021]) recorded the validation-study replication sensitivity of 0.996 (95% CI 0.98-1.00). The team expected real-world sensitivity to be lower. It set targets of ≥ 0.95, with 0.90 as the floor, and required double testing until at least 100 RT-PCR positives were included, with no more than 4 false negatives allowed. It reported that performance "in Venray en Amsterdam erg uiteen liepen" (differed greatly between sites), that about **30% of results were "onduidelijk"** (indeterminate), and it asked for reporting on failed analyses and too-high alcohol readings ([WOO document](https://open.minvws.nl/dossier/VWS-WOO/3551818-1045128-pdo/file/download/document/1ee84816-1426-6ccc-afa6-5d6a8657aabd)). A secondary press report, DutchNews of 15 July 2021 citing Volkskrant, said results at three test centres were so unreliable that most people had to retest; it gives no figures ([DutchNews](https://www.dutchnews.nl/2021/07/coronavirus-breath-test-system-is-not-delivering-desired-results-volkskrant)). No peer-reviewed publication of the verification numbers, and no peer-reviewed version of the preprint, was found. Because the data were not shared, outsiders cannot work out why the test failed.

### No independent replication found

These searches found **no group without Breathomix device or analytics involvement that replicated a specific Sterk-group model**. This is a gap in the searches, not proof that none exists. Erasmus MC ILD work shares Amsterdam co-authors (Moor 2021). Formsma 2026 lists neither Sterk nor de Vries, but it uses the SpiroNose, and any Breathomix analysis role is UNVERIFIED. The LUMC post-COVID paper shares an author, Geelhoed, with the COVID preprint. Other SpiroNose users ran single-centre feasibility studies: lung transplant (PMID 37745948), heart-transplant metabolism (PMID 41214692) and melaNose (PMID 40248699) ([PubMed 37745948](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37745948/)). A Bari group led by former Sterk fellow Dragonieri reported a modest 73.3% leave-one-out accuracy for IPF vs autoimmune UIP with a Cyranose-style eNose, n = 60 ([PubMed 42122346](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42122346/)). No independent replication of the Fens 2009/2011 Cyranose models was found.

## Company interests are disclosed; biological inspiration is absent

### Conflicts of interest as disclosed

The disclosures are explicit and consistent. In Brinkman 2020 (ERJ), "P.J. Sterk ... reports a 4% interest and is a scientific advisor for Breathomix BV, outside the submitted work" ([PubMed 31515400](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31515400/)). In van Bragt 2020/21, "R. de Vries is COO of and has a considerable interest in the start-up company Breathomix BV," and Sterk has "a formally inconsiderable interest" ([PMC7792783](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC7792783)). Disselhorst 2021 describes Breathomix as producing "eNoses linked to an online cloud solution" and "accompanying cloud algorithms," with de Vries holding "a substantial interest" and Sterk "an officially non-substantial interest" ([PubMed 34087572](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34087572/)). Chest 2023 adds that co-authors N. Farzan and Y.W.F. Dagelet "receive personal fees" from Breathomix ([PMC10635840](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10635840)). Licht 2023 adds G. Slingers (personal fees) ([PubMed 36849333](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36849333/)), and Seidl 2026 repeats the de Vries disclosure ([PubMed 41881763](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41881763/)). Boehringer Ingelheim partly sponsored the SpiroNose in the LUMC study ([PubMed 37081422](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37081422/)). The papers call Breathomix a "start-up company." "Spin-off" and "founders" were **not confirmed** in any source. The Chest 2023 phrase "a spin-out of the BreathCloud project" refers to the study, not the company. The company's own site gives no founding date or founders and lists Loosdrecht where the papers say Leiden ([Breathomix about](https://www.breathomix.com/about/)). The academic-to-company credit shift points strongly to Breathomix commercialising the AMC device, but that remains an inference. Many SpiroNose validation papers include an author with a Breathomix interest, which matters when judging independence.

### Not biologically inspired

Every Sterk-group paper examined used conventional chemometrics and statistics: PCA, LDA or canonical discriminant analysis, ROC, PLS-DA, t-tests, linear regression, Ward/K-means/PAM/TDA clustering, mixed models, PERMANOVA and Bayesian networks. A Europe PMC query combining author Sterk PJ with neuromorphic, spiking, bio-inspired or neural-network terms returned one unrelated 2005 Thorax asthma paper (PMID 16061704) ([Europe PMC search](https://europepmc.org/search?query=AUTH%3A%22Sterk%20PJ%22%20AND%20neural%20network)). The furthest step toward ML in this lineage was a collaborator-led random-forest benchmark ([PubMed 37595574](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37595574/)). The nearest thing to an adaptive, biology-like idea is a personalised baseline. Lammers 2021 tracked each sensor's absolute % deviation from that person's own pre-challenge mean, and fluctuations rose one day after rhinovirus challenge in asthma, before symptoms appeared ([PubMed 33704785](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33704785/)). That is simple statistics, not neuromorphic processing. Whatever Breathomix's cloud "advanced signal processing" contains is undisclosed. The full text of all 74 Sterk eNose records was not searched exhaustively.

## Lessons for a low-cost sensor-array e-nose

Each lesson below is tied to its evidence. The BME688 translations are this report's engineering inferences, not anything the sources tested.

| Lesson | Evidence basis | BME688 / sw-enose translation |
|---|---|---|
| Measure ambient air at the same time and subtract it after lag alignment | Low-pass filter, detrend, average references, cross-correlation lag, subtract, detrend; array agreement reached kappa 0.81-0.94 ([JBR2015](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1752-7155/9/4/046001/pdf)) | Second BME688 running the same heater profile on room air. Port the pipeline step by step, and log the chosen filter cutoff and lag window, since the group never published theirs |
| Flow drives raw MOS signal | R² = 0.84 with expiratory flow; 0.19-0.38 L/s recommended; coaching only, no resistor | Add a cheap flow sensor or fixed orifice. Record flow per breath, reject out-of-range breaths with a pre-set rule, and include flow as a covariate |
| Anchor features to manoeuvre events and use ratios | Peak normalised to the "most stable" sensor, plus peak/breath-hold ratio; 13 values per measurement | Time-stamp breath-hold and exhalation end. Normalise each heater step to a pre-chosen stable step and pick that step on pilot data, not validation data. Keep the feature count low |
| Fewer, collinear features overfit easily | Kaiser-criterion PCA fitted on training data only; the group warns that overlapping same-sensor variables overfit ([thesis Ch. 8](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/86066334/Chapter_8.pdf)) | Ten heater steps per sensor add collinearity, not independent chemistry. Fit all reduction inside the training fold |
| Run a QC gas every session, but don't mistake it for calibration | Diffusion test gas daily; no per-device correction; no published unit-to-unit calibration | Use a fixed reference (e.g. a sealed ethanol or calibrated mix) per session, log the response, and test drift explicitly |
| Drift tracks the calendar | Signal associated with measurement date ([PubMed 36501947](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36501947/)) | Randomise class order across days, log timestamps, and test date as a covariate before claiming discrimination |
| Healthy controls and in-sample CV inflate results | AUC 1.00 vs healthy controls; −0.10 to −0.15 from in-set to CV; up to −0.23 more on external data ([PubMed 26669708](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26669708/)) | Report in-sample, CV and held-out results side by side. Use confusable comparators. Freeze the model and threshold before testing |
| Sites and operators break models | 0.92 → 0.80 on a broader population; Venray vs Amsterdam divergence; ~30% indeterminate | Hold out whole devices, sessions and operators. Report failure and indeterminate rates. Power the study on the lower confidence bound |
| Share data | Ministry: Breathomix did not share requested data | Publish raw traces and the processing code, which an open-source project can do and the commercial device did not |

## Conclusion

The Sterk lineage reads best as a long experiment in measurement discipline, not as a catalogue of diagnostic successes. The engineering contributions are concrete and portable: on-line differential sensing against a co-located ambient array, flow-aware manoeuvres, and ratio features that cancel gain differences. Yet the hard problems for a fleet of MOS devices remain undocumented in the open literature: unit-to-unit calibration, sensor identity, heater settings and long-term drift. They now sit inside a commercial cloud. An open-source project can fill exactly that gap by publishing heater profiles, filter parameters, calibration-transfer tests and raw data that the SpiroNose record lacks.

The validation history also sets a realistic target. Every time this group tightened its design, moving from cross-validation to split samples, then to a frozen model on a new population, then to whole held-out centres, then to 1800 devices in public test sites, measured performance fell. The national deployment fell short of its pre-set sensitivity. For sw-enose, internal accuracy is only the first gate. The results that count are those from a frozen pipeline on unseen devices, days and operators, reported together with failure rates. In the evidence gathered here, no SpiroNose model has yet passed that test independently.
